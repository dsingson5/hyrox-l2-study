SkiErg technique & programming

The SkiErg is station 1 — entered on fresh legs but with HR already elevated from run 1, so it sets the tone for the whole race. Power comes from a sequenced chain: tall start with loaded lats, then hip hinge and lat/tricep drive together, trunk compressing over the hips, finishing with a short abdominal/hip snap and a relaxed recovery back to full stretch. The hinge and trunk contribute substantially more force than elbow flexion alone; athletes who pull mainly with the arms tend to fade. On a Concept2 machine, displayed power rises with roughly the cube of pace, so small split improvements demand disproportionately large watt increases. Manage damper/drag, stroke rate and stroke length together, and exit the machine with HR and grip intact for the sleds.

▶Sled push - drive sequence
drag to scrub ↔
Step 1 of 4
A braced forward lean plus relentless leg drive. Almost all concentric (you only push, never lower a load), so it builds power and conditioning with little soreness - the headline Hyrox alternative mode.
Key facts to memorize
  • Station order: Ski is #1 of 8 → run 1 → SkiErg 1000 m. Fatigue arriving here is largely systemic (cardiovascular) rather than local, though upper-body and grip fatigue accumulate during the station itself.
  • Sequence: hinge + lat drive together → trunk compression → short abs/hip finish → relaxed recovery back to full overhead reach.
  • Concept2 relation: watts ≈ 2.80 / t³, where t = seconds per metre. Power therefore scales with roughly the cube of velocity, so pace gains are expensive in watts.
  • Damper (1–10) ≠ drag factor. The damper is an airflow lever; drag factor is the machine-measured flywheel deceleration and is the value that makes resistance comparable across units.
  • Mid-range damper/drag settings are commonly used, but no single optimum is established — high damper raises per-stroke load and forearm/lat fatigue, low damper tends to raise stroke rate and cardiac cost. Test both and individualise.
  • Ski stroke rates in racing are generally higher than rowing rates (rowing typically sits in the 20s–low 30s). Exact ski rates vary widely by athlete; rate alone is not a quality marker.
  • Power = stroke rate × work per stroke. Raising rate only helps if work per stroke does not fall proportionally more — a shortened stroke at high rate can leave output unchanged or lower.
  • Common faults: squatting instead of hinging, early elbow bend, pulling the handles back well past the hips, yanking the handles apart, incomplete overhead reach on recovery, over-gripping.
  • Pacing: hold target 1000 m split, or roughly 1–3 s/500 m slower than a fresh-tested 1000 m as a practical starting point; the ski should not be a personal-best attempt.
  • Exit cost matters: forearm and grip fatigue carry straight into sled push and, more so, sled pull — easing the last 100–150 m is a reasonable tactic if grip is loading up.
  • Interval formats that transfer: 8–10 × 250 m at goal pace, 4–6 × 500 m off short rest, 3 × 1000 m, and run→ski→run compromised-state blocks.
Visual glossary
Drag factor
A machine-measured number reflecting how quickly the flywheel decelerates between strokes. It standardises resistance feel across different ergs, unlike the damper number alone.
Damper setting
The 1–10 lever that changes airflow into the flywheel cage. It influences drag factor but is not itself a unit of resistance.
Stroke rate (spm)
Strokes completed per minute. Total power equals stroke rate multiplied by work done per stroke, so rate alone says nothing about output.
Lat drive
Initiating the pull with shoulder extension driven by the latissimus dorsi and surrounding back musculature rather than with elbow flexion.
Split
Time to cover a reference distance at the current pace — conventionally per 500 m on Concept2 machines.
Compromised-state interval
An interval performed immediately after another modality (e.g. ski straight off a run) to train the transition and pacing under pre-existing fatigue.
Roxzone
The transition area between runs and stations; time spent there counts toward total race time.
Stroke length
The distance the handles travel from the fully stretched overhead position to the finish. Shortening it drops work per stroke even if rate stays high.
Connection to your training

Test your athlete's fresh 1000 m ski, then set a race target modestly slower (about 1–3 s/500 m is a workable starting point) and rehearse it off a run at goal 1 km pace rather than standing rested at the machine. Watch the first 200 m specifically — this is where athletes most often go out several seconds per 500 m too fast on adrenaline and pay for it on the sleds. Coach the exit as part of the station: ease the last 150 m, shake the forearms out in the roxzone, and cue hinge-and-lat drive rather than arm pulling whenever the split drifts while stroke rate climbs.

Running technique under fatigue

HYROX running is never fresh running. Every station leaves its own mechanical debt: the sled push loads the knee extensors and calves in a sustained forward-lean, short-lever drive; the sled pull is a hand-over-hand upper-back and grip effort over a braced, largely isometric lower body; sandbag lunges load the quads, glutes and erectors heavily through range; wall balls and burpee broad jumps drain the same triple-extension chain. The first strides out of the roxzone commonly show reduced cadence, longer ground contact, more trunk flexion, and overstriding with a braking heel strike. Coaching the transition means restoring posture and cadence before restoring speed, and deliberately rehearsing compromised running so the degraded pattern is already familiar and self-correcting on race day.

Key facts to memorize
  • Typical fatigue signature out of a station: ↓ cadence, ↑ ground contact time, ↑ trunk flexion, often ↑ pelvic drop → overstride + heel braking. Magnitude varies widely between athletes.
  • Sled push = high knee-extensor + plantarflexor load in a sustained forward lean → the athlete tends to carry that lean and a short choppy stride into the run; cue tall posture first.
  • Sled pull = predominantly lats, upper back and grip, with the legs braced and largely isometric → less direct corruption of stride mechanics than the push, but grip and postural fatigue still degrade arm carriage and trunk position.
  • Sandbag lunges (100 m) load the quads, glutes and erectors hard through a long range — plausibly one of the most disruptive stations for subsequent run mechanics, though which station hurts most is individual and should be measured, not assumed.
  • Cadence is the fastest lever: hold near the athlete's own habitual cadence even as pace drops — this shortens stride, cuts braking, and demands less peak force from fatigued legs. Habitual cadence is highly individual and rises with speed; the commonly cited '180 spm' is a rough average, not a target.
  • The first 100-200 m out of the roxzone is best treated as a re-patterning window rather than a passing window; a controlled slightly-slower opening often beats surging, though the optimal amount varies by athlete and station.
  • Early-run heaviness is multifactorial — local metabolite accumulation, residual muscle fatigue, and the demands of redistributing blood flow between working muscle groups all plausibly contribute; mechanics typically settle once cadence and breathing stabilise.
  • Roxzone time counts toward the finishing time; sloppy transitions add up meaningfully across the race — walk with purpose rather than stagger-then-sprint.
  • Compromised running is trainable. Both general aerobic and strength-endurance work and specific station→run rehearsal contribute; the specific pairings are worth training in race order, but generic fatigued running is not wasted.
  • Common technique fault chain: quad fatigue → ↓ knee flexion at swing → overstride → ↑ braking impulse → ↑ energy cost → further fatigue.
  • Monitor cadence, GCT and heart-rate drift per run leg. A meaningful cadence decline from the early to the late run legs is a useful practical flag for pacing or durability problems — treat any specific percentage as a working coaching heuristic, not an established cutoff.
Visual glossary
Compromised running
Running performed under pre-existing local muscular fatigue from a preceding non-running task. In HYROX it is the default running state, not a special case.
Roxzone
The transition area between run and station. Time spent there counts toward the finishing time, so it is coached as an active, deliberately paced segment.
Ground contact time (GCT)
Duration each foot spends on the ground per stride. It commonly lengthens under fatigue, and shorter contact times are generally associated with better running economy — an association rather than a simple lever to pull.
Leg stiffness
The spring-like resistance of the lower limb to compression on landing. Fatigue can alter it, but the research is genuinely mixed — decreases, increases and no change have all been reported — so treat it as a concept to understand rather than a metric to chase.
Braking impulse
The backward force generated when the foot lands ahead of the centre of mass. Rises with overstriding and wastes forward momentum.
Cadence
Steps per minute. Holding cadence while allowing stride length to shorten is the simplest way to preserve mechanics as pace drops.
Station-to-run coupling
The trained ability to restore acceptable running mechanics quickly after a specific station. Partly task-specific, so it benefits from rehearsing the actual pairing.
Transition cue stack
A short, fixed sequence of coaching cues an athlete runs through on leaving the roxzone, e.g. posture → breathe → cadence → reach.
Durability
The ability to retain mechanical and physiological efficiency deep into accumulated fatigue, rather than peak fresh capacity.
Connection to your training

Program your athlete's transitions as skill work, not just conditioning: after every sled or lunge set in the build phase, send them straight into 200-400 m at target race pace and score the first 100 m on posture and cadence, not on split. Give them one fixed cue stack — stand tall, exhale hard, quick feet, then lengthen — and drill it until it fires automatically. In race-simulation sessions, use a metronome or cadence alert and hold habitual cadence out of each roxzone; review per-leg cadence and GCT afterwards to find which station consistently breaks that individual's form, then bias practice toward that specific pairing rather than assuming which station is worst.

Cadence, stride length & running economy

Speed = cadence x stride length, so any pace can be produced by many combinations of the two. Most self-selected patterns sit close to the metabolically optimal combination for that individual, but fatigued or undertrained runners tend to drift toward long, low-cadence strides: the foot lands well ahead of the centre of mass with an extended knee, creating a larger horizontal braking impulse, longer ground-contact time and greater vertical oscillation. Raising cadence roughly 5-10% shortens the stride, brings footstrike closer under the hips, reduces braking and shifts load away from the knee and hip toward the ankle, calf and Achilles. Cadence cueing redistributes load; economy itself is improved mainly through heavy strength and plyometric training (tendon and leg-spring stiffness), not by a cue alone.

▶Sprint mechanics — start to cut
drag to scrub ↔
Step 1 of 4
Acceleration is a forward-lean push; max velocity is tall with brief, straight-down ground force; agility is a controlled sink-and-plant to redirect.
Key facts to memorize
  • Speed = cadence x stride length; cadence in steps/min (spm), stride length taken here as distance per step.
  • Typical observed range: recreational runners ~150-170 spm, well-trained/elite ~175-190+; '180 for everyone' is a myth — optimal cadence varies with speed, leg length and stature.
  • As pace increases, speed is gained mainly by lengthening the stride at low-to-moderate speeds; cadence becomes the dominant contributor only as the runner approaches near-maximal sprint speeds.
  • Overstriding = footstrike far ahead of COM with an extended knee → larger braking impulse, longer contact time, higher impact loading rate, poorer economy.
  • Cadence increased ~5-10% at a fixed pace → shorter stride, less vertical oscillation of the COM, lower patellofemoral and hip load; higher load per minute at the ankle/Achilles/calf.
  • Reducing cadence (longer stride) shifts load the other way — more knee and hip, less Achilles — which can be used deliberately when managing site-specific injury.
  • Running economy = the oxygen or energy cost of running at a given submaximal speed; among athletes matched for VO2max and lactate threshold, the more economical runner is generally faster.
  • Heavy resistance training (commonly ~80%+ 1RM) combined with plyometrics improves running economy in most trials by roughly 2-8% over about 6-14 weeks, attributed largely to greater tendon/leg stiffness and shorter ground-contact time, and typically without meaningful gains in body mass.
  • Shoe mass matters: economy cost is often estimated at roughly 1% per extra 100 g per shoe; modern carbon-plated 'super' shoes have been shown to improve economy by a few percent in lab testing.
  • Fatigue commonly degrades running form — cadence tends to fall while contact time and vertical oscillation rise (individual responses vary). HYROX running is nearly always performed pre-fatigued, so form under fatigue has to be trained, not assumed.
Visual glossary
Cadence
Step frequency, usually expressed as steps per minute counting both legs. Halve it to get strides per minute, since one stride = two steps.
Braking impulse
The backward (decelerating) horizontal force applied over the early part of stance. It grows when the foot lands far ahead of the centre of mass.
Running economy
The oxygen or energy cost of running at a fixed submaximal speed. Lower cost at the same pace means better economy.
Leg stiffness
The ratio of peak force to how much the leg-spring compresses during stance. Higher stiffness means less compression for a given load, and is associated with shorter ground contact and better elastic energy return.
Ground contact time (GCT)
Time the foot spends on the ground each step. At a given pace, shorter GCT tends to accompany higher cadence and is often associated with better economy, though it is not a guarantee of it.
Vertical oscillation
Up-and-down travel of the centre of mass per stride. Excess oscillation represents energy spent moving upward rather than forward.
Overstriding
Landing with the foot notably ahead of the hips and a relatively straight knee, producing a braking spike and, in most runners, a harder heel-first collision.
Compromised running
Running performed on pre-fatigued muscles immediately after a strength- or grip-heavy task — the characteristic running condition in HYROX.
Roxzone
The transition between a run and a station. That transition time counts toward the total race time, and it is where cadence and posture have to be re-established.
Connection to your training

Give your athlete a re-entry cadence cue for the first 100-150 m out of every roxzone — a metronome or a counted 3-step rhythm — because cadence and posture are typically the first things to degrade after heavy stations such as the sled push and sandbag lunges, and a long, braking stride there costs seconds on each of the eight 1 km runs. Build the underlying economy separately: two heavy lower-body sessions per week (squat, deadlift, split squat in the ~80%+ 1RM range, low reps) plus short plyometrics performed on fresh legs. Reserve compromised-running work (station into 400-1000 m at race pace) for testing whether that form holds under fatigue. Progress cadence changes gradually — a 5-10% increase adds load to the Achilles and calf, so build it over weeks rather than in a single session, and screen for existing Achilles symptoms first.

Motor learning & skill acquisition

Motor learning is the set of practice-driven processes that produce a relatively permanent change in the capability for skilled movement — distinct from performance, which is what you observe on the day and is confounded by fatigue, arousal and the feedback currently being given. Athletes are commonly described as passing through cognitive (large errors, heavy attentional demand, verbal-motor problem solving), associative (errors shrinking, refinement, self-detection emerging) and autonomous (largely automated, low attentional cost, more robust under load) stages; these are descriptive phases on a continuum, not sharp thresholds, and the autonomous stage requires extensive practice that many athletes never fully reach for complex skills. Transfer is governed by specificity: skills carry over to the extent practice matches the competition's coordination pattern, load, tempo and — critically for HYROX — its fatigue and decision context. Practice conditions that feel worse (variable, random, distributed, reduced-frequency feedback) tend to depress acute performance while improving retention and transfer. Fatigue degrades coordination and the athlete's own error detection, so new patterns are best taught fresh and stress-proofed later.

Key facts to memorize
  • Learning = durable capability change, inferred from delayed retention + transfer tests; performance = what you see today, contaminated by fatigue/arousal/feedback.
  • Fitts & Posner stages: cognitive → associative → autonomous; across stages automation ↑, attentional demand ↓, within-athlete movement variability ↓. Treat them as a continuum, not discrete levels.
  • Autonomous stage frees attention → the athlete can pace, count reps and read the roxzone instead of thinking about the movement itself.
  • Blocked/constant practice: ↑ acute performance, typically ↓ retention. Random/variable practice: ↓ acute, typically ↑ retention + transfer — the contextual interference effect.
  • The contextual interference benefit is moderated by skill level and task complexity: rank novices and very complex tasks often need blocked practice first, with randomisation introduced once a basic pattern exists.
  • Feedback: high-frequency or concurrent KP can create dependency on the external signal (the guidance hypothesis). Faded, bandwidth, summary and self-controlled feedback generally favour retention.
  • External focus cues ("drive the floor away", "send the ball to the target") tend to outperform internal body-part cues for both output and retention, though effect sizes vary by task and expertise.
  • Specificity of transfer: carryover scales with shared coordination pattern, velocity, load and physiological state — practising skills only when fresh under-prepares race execution.
  • Fatigue effects: ↓ proprioceptive acuity, ↓ force control, altered joint kinematics, ↓ ability to detect one's own errors → new motor programs are encoded poorly, and injury risk rises, under high fatigue.
  • Practical rule: acquire technique at low fatigue early in the session; stress-test an already-acquired skill under fatigue later.
  • Cognitive-stage athletes need a demonstration, a clear reference model and very few cues (a common coaching heuristic is one cue per set); autonomous athletes generally respond better to task constraints and environment design than to more verbal instruction.
Visual glossary
Motor learning
Practice-driven processes producing a relatively permanent improvement in the capability to produce a movement. Assessed by retention and transfer, not by a single good session.
Performance
Observable movement quality/output at one moment in time. It can rise or fall from fatigue, arousal or feedback without any underlying change in learning.
Contextual interference
The learning benefit from mixing tasks or variants within a practice block rather than repeating one. It slows in-session performance but typically improves retention and transfer; the effect is smaller or reversed for true novices on complex tasks.
Positive transfer
Prior practice on one task improving a target task, driven by shared coordination pattern, load, velocity and context.
Knowledge of results (KR) / knowledge of performance (KP)
KR is feedback about the outcome (time, reps, metres); KP is feedback about how the movement was executed (technique).
Guidance hypothesis
The explanation that frequent or concurrent feedback guides performance in the moment but blocks development of the athlete's own error-detection, so learning suffers once feedback is withdrawn.
Fatigued-skill practice
A practitioner label (not a formal textbook term) for deliberately rehearsing an already-acquired skill in a pre-fatigued state so technique holds at race-relevant physiological cost.
External focus of attention
Directing attention to the movement's effect on the environment or implement rather than to body segments, which typically improves both output and retention.
Autonomous stage
The final described learning stage where execution is largely automatic, error rate is low, and spare attention is available for pacing, tactics and self-regulation. Reached only after extensive practice.
Retention test
A delayed re-test after a practice block, conducted without feedback, used to separate genuine learning from temporary performance boosts.
Transfer test
A test of the skill in a novel or race-relevant condition (different load, tempo or fatigue state) used to check whether the learning generalises beyond the practice context.
Connection to your training

Teach any new HYROX pattern — burpee broad jump rhythm, sandbag lunge stride, wall ball catch-to-squat timing — early in the session, before meaningful fatigue accumulates, using one external cue at a time and letting the athlete self-report errors before you correct them. Keep early reps blocked and low-volume until the basic pattern exists, then introduce variability and mixed ordering. Once the pattern is reliable, deliberately relocate it behind a hard run or a sled so you can see whether it survives race fatigue; that fatigued rehearsal is skill-proofing, not skill-teaching. If technique holds when fresh and collapses only under load, the limiter is capacity or pacing rather than motor learning, and your intervention should shift accordingly.

Lactate, buffering & fatigue

Lactate is not a waste product and not the direct cause of acidosis. Fast glycolysis produces pyruvate faster than mitochondria can accept it; the LDH reaction (pyruvate + NADH + H+ → lactate + NAD+) regenerates NAD+ so glycolytic ATP flow continues. Lactate then shuttles — cytosol → mitochondria, fast fibres → slow fibres, muscle → blood → heart, liver, brain, other muscle — as a fuel and a signalling molecule (gluconeogenic substrate, mitochondrial biogenesis signal). The H+ load that accompanies hard work comes chiefly from ATP hydrolysis during high glycolytic flux, and is buffered by bicarbonate, intramuscular phosphates, proteins and carnosine. Blood lactate is a marker of the balance between appearance and disappearance, not the toxin itself.

Key facts to memorize
  • Lactate production ≠ acidosis. H+ arises mainly from ATP hydrolysis when glycolytic flux is high; the LDH step itself consumes a proton.
  • LDH: pyruvate + NADH + H+ → lactate + NAD+ → regenerating NAD+ keeps glycolysis (and its ATP output) running.
  • Cell-to-cell and intracellular lactate shuttles: MCT transporters move lactate into oxidative fibres, heart, liver (Cori cycle) and brain, where it is oxidised or reconverted.
  • Blood lactate = appearance minus disappearance. A steady value means matched rates, not 'no lactate being made'.
  • Thresholds: LT1 (first sustained rise above baseline) and LT2 (highest sustainable steady state). LT2, MLSS and critical power are related but NOT identical — critical power typically sits slightly above MLSS.
  • There is no universal threshold lactate value. MLSS commonly falls somewhere around 2–6 mmol/L and is highly individual; the familiar fixed 4 mmol/L 'OBLA' is a testing convention, not a physiological constant.
  • Buffers: bicarbonate (extracellular); inorganic phosphate, proteins and carnosine (intracellular). β-alanine supplementation raises muscle carnosine over several weeks; sodium bicarbonate acutely raises extracellular buffering.
  • Fatigue during high-intensity work is multifactorial: inorganic phosphate accumulation is now considered a major direct impairer of cross-bridge force and Ca2+ handling, with acidosis contributing alongside it plus rising afferent feedback and RPE. Classic textbook claims that H+ alone explains the force loss are overstated.
  • HYROX stations such as the sled push, sled pull, burpee broad jumps and wall balls drive high glycolytic flux through large muscle groups; sustained high intramuscular tension in the sleds and wall balls also restricts local perfusion, so lactate and H+ rise sharply.
  • The run after a station is the clearance phase: submaximal aerobic work oxidises lactate. Start that run too hard and appearance keeps outpacing disappearance.
  • Better-trained athletes clear faster (greater MCT1 expression, mitochondrial density and capillarity). Raising the pace at which lactate stays in steady state — not suppressing lactate — is the adaptation target.
Visual glossary
Lactate shuttle
The movement of lactate from where it is produced to sites that oxidise or reconvert it — within a cell, between fibre types, or between organs via the blood.
MCT1 / MCT4
Monocarboxylate transporters that co-transport lactate with a proton across membranes. MCT4 predominates in glycolytic fibres and favours export; MCT1 predominates in oxidative tissue and favours uptake.
LT2 / MLSS
The highest intensity at which blood lactate appearance and disappearance still balance over a sustained effort. Above it, lactate and H+ climb progressively until work rate falls.
Critical power (CP)
The asymptote of the power–duration relationship, marking the boundary of the severe-intensity domain. Closely related to MLSS but usually a slightly higher intensity — the two should not be treated as the same test.
Buffering capacity
The tissue's ability to absorb H+ without a large pH fall — bicarbonate outside the cell; phosphates, proteins and carnosine inside.
Carnosine
A muscle dipeptide that buffers intramuscular H+. Muscle content is raised by several weeks of consistent β-alanine supplementation.
Cori cycle
Hepatic conversion of blood lactate back to glucose, which can return to the muscle — lactate recycled as fuel rather than discarded.
Exercise-induced acidosis
The fall in muscle and blood pH during high-intensity work, driven by H+ accumulation. Associated with rising lactate, but not caused by lactate.
Lactate clearance
The rate at which lactate is taken up and oxidised or reconverted elsewhere. It shapes how quickly an athlete recovers between hard efforts.
Connection to your training

Coach the roxzone exit, not just the station. After a sled push or wall balls an athlete leaves with a large local H+ and lactate load, so the opening stretch of the next run should be deliberately conservative before settling onto target pace — that submaximal aerobic window is where lactate is actually shuttled and oxidised. Exact numbers are a coaching heuristic rather than a validated prescription: something on the order of 150–200 m at roughly 15–30 s/km slower than goal pace is a sensible starting point to test and individualise. Build the clearance machinery directly with threshold and sub-threshold running to raise LT2, plus repeated station-into-run compromised blocks so the athlete rehearses running well while acidotic.

1 month backSports Medicine, Recovery & InjuryModule 2 · Sports Medicine: Optimizing Health, Recovery & Performance

Recovery physiology & sleep

Recovery is not one process but four running on different clocks: substrate (muscle glycogen, hours→24 h), fluid and electrolytes (hours), tissue repair of myofibrils and connective tissue (1–3+ days), and central/autonomic restoration of drive, mood and motor control (hours→weeks). Training supplies the stimulus; recovery converts it into adaptation — so the coach programmes the dose AND the restoration. Sleep is the highest-leverage modality: slow-wave sleep carries the largest nocturnal growth-hormone pulse and supports immune function and physical restoration, while REM consolidates motor skill and regulates mood. Chronic restriction is associated with higher injury and illness risk, higher RPE at a fixed workload, and slower reaction time. Passive modalities (CWI, compression, massage) are secondary, largely perceptual, and CWI used habitually can blunt strength/hypertrophy adaptation.

Key facts to memorize
  • Substrate: muscle glycogen resynthesis runs ≈5%/h with adequate CHO (up to ~7%/h with aggressive early feeding); full restoration ~20–24 h. Fast turnaround (<8 h) → 1.0–1.2 g/kg/h CHO for the first ~4 h. Tissue: protein ~1.6–2.2 g/kg/day in ~0.3–0.4 g/kg doses — daily total matters far more than the narrow 'anabolic window'.
  • Rehydration: replace ~125–150% of the fluid deficit WITH sodium — plain water alone drops plasma osmolality, drives diuresis, and leaves restoration incomplete.
  • Sleep architecture: ~90 min cycles, 4–6/night. SWS is front-loaded (largest GH pulse, immune and physical restoration); REM is back-loaded (motor consolidation, mood) — so cutting the last 2 h of sleep removes disproportionately more REM.
  • Athlete target ~7–9 h/night, with many practitioners pushing 8–10 h in heavy blocks. In the adolescent-athlete literature, sleeping <8 h/night was associated with roughly 1.7× the odds of injury — an association, not a demonstrated cause.
  • Sleep extension (raising time-in-bed toward ~10 h for several weeks) has improved sprint times, reaction time, shooting accuracy and mood in trained athletes — the best-evidenced recovery intervention available.
  • Naps: 20–30 min avoids sleep inertia; ~90 min completes a full cycle. Keep naps to the early afternoon, several hours before bedtime, so overnight sleep pressure stays intact.
  • CWI ~11–15 °C for ~10–15 min reduces soreness and perceived fatigue; genuinely useful in congested competition and multi-day settings where the goal is next-day performance.
  • CWI used regularly AFTER resistance work blunts hypertrophy/strength adaptation (attenuated mTOR-pathway and satellite-cell signalling) → avoid it in strength-building blocks; endurance adaptation appears less affected, though the evidence there is mixed.
  • Compression and massage: fairly consistent reductions in perceived soreness, small and inconsistent effects on objective force restoration — low-cost, low-risk, not a substitute for sleep or fuel.
  • Active recovery clears blood lactate faster, but lactate clearance ≠ recovery of force or glycogen; its real value is blood flow, mobility and psychological reset.
Visual glossary
Slow-wave sleep (SWS)
The deepest non-REM stage, concentrated in the first half of the night. Associated with the largest growth-hormone pulse, immune function and physical restoration.
REM sleep
Rapid-eye-movement sleep, concentrated in the later cycles. Supports motor-skill consolidation, learning and emotional regulation.
Sleep inertia
Transient grogginess and impaired reaction time on waking from deep sleep. Why naps are kept to 20–30 min or extended to a full ~90 min cycle.
Sleep extension
Deliberately increasing habitual time in bed above normal for a training or competition block, as an active intervention rather than simply avoiding deprivation.
Cold water immersion (CWI)
Submersion in roughly 11–15 °C water for ~10–15 min post-exercise to reduce perceived soreness and thermal load; may suppress the inflammatory signalling that drives strength adaptation.
Blunted adaptation
When a recovery intervention suppresses the very stress signal the training was meant to create, reducing the long-term training effect despite the athlete feeling better acutely.
Active recovery
Low-intensity aerobic work (typically well below threshold) after or between hard efforts, used to promote blood flow and clearance without adding meaningful training stress.
Functional overreaching
A short-term performance dip from deliberately heavy loading that supercompensates into a gain once recovery is applied. Becomes non-functional overreaching or overtraining if restoration never arrives.
Connection to your training

In a HYROX build, treat sleep as programmed work: audit your athlete's actual time-in-bed before you add a single interval session, and in a peak block push toward 8–10 h plus a 20–30 min early-afternoon nap on double days. Save cold water immersion for congested weeks and race-week turnarounds — do not let them ice down after the sled and lunge strength work that is meant to build the qualities those stations demand. After a full simulation, prioritise the things that actually restore: if the next hard session falls inside ~8 h, feed 1.0–1.2 g/kg/h carbohydrate for four hours; otherwise a normal high-CHO meal pattern across the day is enough. Replace ~125–150% of body-mass loss with sodium-containing fluid, then programme an easy aerobic shakeout the next morning rather than a massage table.

Q4recallSports Medicine, Recovery & Injury
By what mechanism does habitual post-lifting CWI blunt strength gains?
How sure are you?
Answer
It attenuates the acute inflammatory and anabolic signalling cascade (reduced mTOR-pathway activity and satellite-cell activity) that translates the mechanical stimulus into hypertrophy — you remove the signal along with the soreness.

Q8recallSports Medicine, Recovery & Injury
Why is a 45-minute nap often a worse choice than a 25-minute or 90-minute one?
How sure are you?
Answer
A 45 min nap tends to wake the athlete out of slow-wave sleep → sleep inertia (grogginess, slow reaction time) that can persist 20–30 min. 20–30 min stays in lighter sleep; ~90 min completes a full cycle and wakes them near its end.

Q9appliedBiomechanics & Technique
Why is '180 spm is the correct cadence' bad coaching?
How sure are you?
Answer
Optimal cadence varies with pace, leg length, stature and training status, and a runner's self-selected cadence is usually already close to metabolically optimal. Prescribe a relative change (about 5-10%) from the athlete's own baseline rather than an absolute universal target.

Q11appliedBiomechanics & Technique
An athlete raises cadence 8% at the same pace. Which structures get more load and which get less?
How sure are you?
Answer
Less load at the knee and hip, because the stride shortens and vertical COM displacement drops. More load at the ankle, calf and Achilles, because there are more ground contacts per minute.

Q12appliedBiomechanics & Technique
Give an external-focus cue for the sled push and state why it is preferred.
How sure are you?
Answer
"Drive the floor backwards" or "send the sled to the far line" instead of "squeeze your quads". External focus promotes automatic self-organisation and tends to produce higher force output and better retention than internal body-part cues, though the size of the advantage varies by task and expertise.

Q13recallBiomechanics & Technique
Define the specificity principle as it applies to transfer, with a HYROX example.
How sure are you?
Answer
Transfer scales with similarity in coordination pattern, load, velocity and physiological state. Practising farmers carry at race load with race grip and pre-fatigued forearms transfers well; light, unfatigued walks with dumbbells transfer far less.
