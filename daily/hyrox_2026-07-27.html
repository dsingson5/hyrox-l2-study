Exam synthesis — periodization, concurrent training & peaking

Periodization is the deliberate sequencing of training emphasis across a macrocycle, and the honest reading of the comparative literature is that structure beats no structure, while differences between named models — linear, block, undulating — tend to be smaller than the effects of adherence and sensible load management. Polarised distribution describes how intensity is spread, not how phases are sequenced, and the two are often confused. A practical HYROX macrocycle runs general preparation (maximal strength plus aerobic base), specific preparation (station endurance and compromised running), competition (race simulations and a taper cutting volume roughly 40–60% while holding intensity and frequency for one to two weeks), then transition. Concurrent-training interference — endurance blunting strength adaptation, with a molecular story that remains contested — matters mainly at high endurance volumes and with poor session sequencing, and is managed through ordering, separation and off-feet modalities rather than avoided outright.

Key facts to memorize
  • Comparative trials tend to show that any structured periodized plan beats unstructured training, while differences between the named models are small and usually smaller than the effect of adherence.
  • Linear periodization progresses from high-volume/low-intensity toward low-volume/high-intensity across long sequential phases.
  • Block periodization concentrates one dominant quality per mesocycle (commonly accumulation, transmutation, realisation) and relies on residual training effects to hold the others.
  • Daily or weekly undulating periodization (DUP) varies intensity and volume targets within the week, which suits concurrent-demand sports like HYROX that must keep several qualities warm at once.
  • Polarised (~80% easy / ~20% hard) is an intensity-distribution strategy, not a periodization model — a classic exam confusion.
  • A practical HYROX macrocycle runs general prep (strength + aerobic base), specific prep (station endurance + compromised running), competition (race sims + taper), then transition.
  • The interference effect is the blunting of strength and hypertrophy adaptations by concurrent endurance training; the molecular explanation (often framed as AMPK–mTOR crosstalk) is plausible but not settled.
  • Interference matters most at high endurance volumes, when same-muscle endurance work immediately precedes strength training, and when key lifting is done glycogen-depleted.
  • Practical interference mitigation: lift first when strength is the priority, separate sessions by roughly six hours where possible (a heuristic, not a published law), and push some endurance volume to low-impact off-feet modalities.
  • The textbook taper cuts training volume by roughly 40–60% over one to two weeks while maintaining intensity and frequency, typically yielding small but meaningful performance gains of around 1–3%.
  • The most common taper error is cutting intensity rather than volume, which lets race-pace sharpness fade exactly when it is needed.
  • An A/B/C race hierarchy assigns the full taper to A races, a 3–7 day mini-taper to B races, and trains through C races as quality sessions.
  • Functional overreaching is a planned short-term overload that rebounds to supercompensation after recovery; a plan should schedule it deliberately with the recovery week written in advance, never discover it by accident.
  • Strength decays over weeks rather than days, so low-volume, moderate-to-high-intensity weekly touches are enough to hold it between races and through the competition phase.
Visual glossary
Macrocycle
The full planning span (typically a season or year) containing preparation, competition and transition phases.
Mesocycle
A block of roughly 2–6 weeks with one dominant training emphasis, the building unit of a periodized plan.
Block periodization
A model that concentrates a single quality per mesocycle in sequence (accumulation, transmutation, realisation), relying on residual training effects to maintain the qualities not currently emphasised.
Undulating periodization (DUP)
A model that varies intensity and volume targets within the week or day rather than across long phases; well suited to sports that must train several qualities concurrently.
Polarised intensity distribution
Spending roughly 80% of endurance training time at low intensity and about 20% at high intensity; it describes the intensity mix, not the phase sequence, so it is not itself a periodization model.
Interference effect
The attenuation of strength and hypertrophy adaptations when high volumes of endurance training run concurrently, strongest when endurance work loads the same muscles immediately before lifting.
Residual training effect
The period a fitness quality is retained after dedicated training for it stops; strength persists for weeks, while speed and anaerobic qualities fade faster.
Taper
A pre-race reduction of training volume by roughly 40–60% over one to two weeks while maintaining intensity and frequency, allowing fatigue to fall faster than fitness.
Functional overreaching
A planned short-term overload producing temporary performance decline that rebounds above baseline after scheduled recovery; distinct from non-functional overreaching, where the decrement persists without payoff.

Classifying races by priority to decide taper depth: full taper for A races, mini-taper for B races, and training through C races.
Connection to your training

If they lift and run on the same day, fix the ordering — strength first when strength is the priority, or roughly six hours' separation where life allows — and move some easy volume to the SkiErg or bike if their legs are chronically heavy. Draft the race hierarchy now: one A race gets the full one-to-two-week taper, B races get 3–5 day mini-tapers, C races are trained through. Finally, write the taper arithmetic into the plan in advance — peak weekly volume, the 40–60% cut, and the short race-pace sessions that preserve intensity — so race week is executed, not improvised.

Exam synthesis — performance pillars, off-feet conditioning & race execution

Race craft is the synthesis layer that turns fitness into a finishing time, and it rests on three ledgers a coach should hold simultaneously: running time, station time and roxzone time, because total race time is simply their sum. The format itself — eight 1 km runs alternating with eight fixed-order stations — rewards athletes who know each station's demand fingerprint: SkiErg and Row are paceable engine work, the sleds, carries and lunges tax strength-endurance, while wall balls and burpee broad jumps punish rep integrity under fatigue. Pacing doctrine tends to favour even or slightly negative run splits with strict first-run discipline, since early enthusiasm is repaid with interest as compromised running. Roxzone time is usually the cheapest time on course, because it responds mainly to rehearsal and logistics rather than added fitness. Off-feet conditioning (erg and bike work) supplies aerobic volume without impact cost, prescribed by split targets rather than feel — a practical heuristic, not a published model.

Key facts to memorize
  • The race structure is eight 1 km runs alternating with eight stations in a fixed order — SkiErg, Sled Push, Sled Pull, Burpee Broad Jumps, Row, Farmers Carry, Sandbag Lunges, Wall Balls; loads and rep counts are division- and season-dependent (Doubles split station work between partners and Relay formats differ again), so check the current rulebook — the format and rules lesson covers the numbers.
  • Demand fingerprints: SkiErg and Row are pure-engine stations (paceable, low failure risk); Sled Push, Sled Pull, Farmers Carry and Sandbag Lunges are strength-endurance stations; Wall Balls and Burpee Broad Jumps are rep-integrity stations where form breakdown costs no-reps and failure sets.
  • Roxzone time is usually the cheapest place on the course to save minutes: it responds mainly to transition rehearsal, station-order familiarity and logistics planning rather than to added fitness — fatigue does slow roxzone pace somewhat, but rehearsal and logistics are the dominant levers.
  • Community race-data analyses of published splits suggest total roxzone time of roughly four to six minutes for well-drilled athletes versus eight or more for unpractised amateurs — treat these as illustrative rather than official figures, but the gap is largely pure race craft.
  • First-run discipline is the core pacing rule: going out faster than goal pace on run 1 tends to cost more time later through compromised running than it ever gains.
  • Well-paced HYROX athletes aim for even or slightly negative run splits across the eight runs, whereas a fading positive-split profile usually signals an early pacing error rather than poor endurance.
  • Wall Balls and Burpee Broad Jumps are where failure sets occur, and a planned break scheme (moderate sets with short scheduled rests taken before failure) tends to beat unbroken efforts that collapse.
  • Runner-profile athletes typically bleed time on the sleds, lunges and carries; strength-profile athletes bleed time on the back-half runs; the hybrid profile concedes the least anywhere.
  • Off-feet conditioning on the SkiErg, rower and bike adds aerobic volume without impact cost, which protects tissue during high-run blocks, niggle management and return-to-training phases.
  • Erg work is prescribed by split targets (time per 500 m), and because power scales with roughly the cube of speed, small split improvements demand disproportionately large increases in watts.
  • The damper on an erg sets drag factor — how much air the flywheel works against per stroke, i.e. the gearing and feel — not the workout intensity, which comes only from the athlete's power output.
  • Compromised-pair (couplet) training — a station straight into a run, or a run straight into a station — is arguably the most race-specific repeatable conditioning unit short of a full simulation, because it rehearses the exact transition demand of the event.
  • Race-model arithmetic is simply: target time = run budget + station budget + roxzone budget; coach the cheapest gains first, which are usually roxzone and pacing errors, not fitness.
Visual glossary
Roxzone
The transition area between runs and stations; time spent there counts toward the finish and is reduced mainly by rehearsal and familiarity, with fitness playing only a supporting role.
Compromised running
Running on legs pre-fatigued by station work; the defining demand of HYROX and the reason pacing errors compound.
Demand fingerprint
Shorthand for a station's dominant quality — pure engine, strength-endurance, or technique/rep-integrity — used to target preparation and predict where an athlete loses time.
Negative split
Covering the later portion of a race faster than the earlier portion; in HYROX, holding even-to-faster run splits across runs one to eight.
Failure set
A set taken to muscular failure mid-station that forces a long unplanned rest; most race-costly on Wall Balls and Burpee Broad Jumps.
Split (erg)
Time per 500 m on the SkiErg or rower; the standard prescription and pacing unit for erg work.
Drag factor
The measure of flywheel air resistance set by the damper; it determines the gearing and feel of each stroke, not the intensity of the workout.
Compromised pair (couplet)
A training unit pairing a station with a run in either order to rehearse race-specific transitions and pre-fatigue.
Athlete profiling
Classifying an athlete as runner-dominant, strength-dominant or hybrid to predict where race time will be lost and to direct training priorities.
Race model
A simple arithmetic plan that splits a target finish time into run, station and roxzone budgets, then converts each budget into pace and split targets.
Connection to your training

This week, build each athlete a one-page race model: split their target finish time into run, station and roxzone budgets, then work backwards to a per-kilometre pace and per-station split targets. Rehearse the roxzone deliberately — station-order recitation, a walked transition plan and one timed race-flow simulation — because that time is close to free. Programme one compromised-pair session matched to each athlete's weakest fingerprint (for example sled push into 400–800 m repeats at race pace for runner profiles, or station-into-run couplets emphasising back-half run pace for strength profiles). Prescribe erg sessions by 500 m split targets so the SkiErg and Row become controlled, pre-planned stations rather than emotional ones.

Exam synthesis — mechanics, skill acquisition & running technique

This synthesis day binds three threads the assessment is likely to mix freely: mechanics (days 9–12), motor learning (27–29) and running technique (30–33). The unifying idea is impulse–momentum thinking: every station is a problem of building or preserving momentum with a limited force–time budget — a sled start demands one long, continuous impulse from standstill, wall balls and burpee jumps demand small rhythmic impulses repeated at cadence, and running rewards minimising braking impulse per step. A useful way to file faults is into kinetic-chain energy-leak families — hip drop, trunk collapse, overstriding — each exposed by a predictable station or state. The coaching layer then selects learning conditions: blocked practice with rich knowledge of performance for novices and re-learning; random-variable practice under fatigue, external cues and faded feedback for race preparation, because contextual interference tends to trade today's performance for tomorrow's retention and transfer.

🦴Lever classifier — predict the class
The diagram shows a biceps curl: elbow = fulcrum, biceps = effort, dumbbell in the hand = load. Which lever class is this?
Most human limbs are 3rd-class levers. Order along the bone: fulcrum (joint) → effort (muscle inserting close to the joint) → load (weight far out in the hand/foot). Why it matters: a short effort arm vs a long load arm means the muscle must generate several times the external load in force — a force disadvantage traded for a speed & range-of-motion advantage at the hand/foot. That trade is exactly why a small range of muscle shortening produces a large, fast limb movement (your sprint stride, your sled drive).

Greatest at ~90° (forearm horizontal). Torque = force × perpendicular moment arm. Key facts to memorize
  • Impulse is net force multiplied by the time it acts (the area under the force–time curve) and, by the impulse–momentum theorem, equals the change in momentum it produces.
  • A sled start from standstill is impulse-limited: with no momentum to preserve, the athlete must accumulate a large force–time area before movement begins, so a continuous lean-and-drive beats repeated stop–start efforts.
  • Wall balls and burpee jumps sit at the opposite end of the impulse problem — brief impulses repeated at cadence — so every pause or reset re-pays the cost of accelerating body and ball from rest.
  • A useful way to file kinetic-chain faults is into three energy-leak families — contralateral hip drop, trunk collapse and overstriding — a teaching heuristic rather than a formal taxonomy, exposed respectively by single-leg work (sandbag lunges, fatigued running), anteriorly loaded stations (wall balls, sled push) and late-race running.
  • Blocked practice (AAA-BBB) tends to produce better performance within the session, while random practice (ABC-BCA) tends to produce better retention and transfer — the contextual interference effect.
  • Contextual interference is thought to work because task-switching forces the learner to reconstruct the action plan on every attempt and to elaborate on differences between tasks.
  • External cues, which direct attention to the movement's effect on the environment, tend to outperform internal body-focused cues for performance and learning — Wulf's constrained action hypothesis — though the advantage is fairly consistent in direction only; the effect is modest and some recent replications are mixed.
  • Knowledge of performance (KP) describes movement quality ('your hips rose before your chest'), whereas knowledge of results (KR) describes the outcome ('that set was 15 seconds slower') — a favourite exam confusion.
  • Feedback frequency should generally be faded as skill develops, because constant after-every-rep feedback breeds dependency and blocks the athlete's own error detection.
  • Running speed equals cadence multiplied by stride length — taken here, per this site's convention, as the distance covered per step — so at a fixed speed any cadence increase must be paid for with a proportionally shorter stride.
  • A modest cadence increase of around 5% — not a universal 180 steps per minute — tends to reduce overstriding, braking impulse and per-step loading, making it the safest first running-economy lever.
  • Reducing vertical oscillation and braking are honest but modest economy levers, and large deliberate technique overhauls often cost economy in the short term before any benefit appears.
  • Under fatigue, running form tends to degrade in a recognisable signature — cadence falls, ground contact time lengthens and trunk flexion increases, with stride length also shortening as pace fades — which is why restoring cadence is the first cue out of the roxzone (the transition zone), and why the compromised state must itself be rehearsed.
  • Race preparation should progress practice conditions from blocked and fresh towards random-variable and fatigued (run–station couplets), matching specificity of both skill and physiological state.

Impulse
The product of net force and the time over which it acts — the area under the force–time curve. By the impulse–momentum theorem it equals the change in momentum it produces.

A point in the kinetic chain where force is lost to unwanted motion (hip drop, trunk collapse) instead of being transmitted to the task.

Initial ground contact too far ahead of the centre of mass, which increases braking impulse and per-step load.
Connection to your training

This week, run one video session per athlete and file every fault under an energy-leak family rather than a station-specific note: hip drop (lunges, fatigued running), trunk collapse (wall balls, sled), overstriding (late-race running). Fix each with one external cue, not three internal ones. Then audit your practice schedule against athlete level: give your newest athlete blocked sets of their weakest station with frequent knowledge of performance; give race-ready athletes shuffled run–station couplets with feedback only every few rounds. Finally, rehearse degradation — finish one session with a 1 km run at race pace straight into their leakiest station, and cue cadence, not stride length, when form fades.

1 week backCoaching, Mindset & IndividualisationModule 10 · Personalized Performance Coaching

Coaching decisions — applied case studies

Case work is a diagnostic discipline: identify the rate-limiter, then apply the smallest intervention that moves it. HYROX performance decomposes into run economy/aerobic ceiling, station-specific strength-endurance, compromised-running tolerance, roxzone efficiency, and pacing discipline. Archetypes recur — strong-but-slow (aerobic ceiling caps everything), runner-fails-on-sleds (a force/strength deficit against a high-friction horizontal push and pull), the recurring calf (load-tolerance and tissue-capacity failure, not a flexibility problem), the first-timer peaking (taper and race-execution, not fitness). Each demands a different lever: aerobic volume, targeted heavy strength, progressive calf/tendon loading, or rehearsal plus deload. Wrong lever → training stress without transfer. Note that archetypes are a starting hypothesis for triage, not a diagnosis; athletes commonly present with mixed or overlapping limiters.

Key facts to memorize
  • Race format anchor: 8 × 1 km runs alternating with 8 stations in fixed order — SkiErg, Sled Push, Sled Pull, Burpee Broad Jump, Row, Farmers Carry, Sandbag Lunges, Wall Balls. Diagnosis starts from where in that sequence time is actually lost.
  • Diagnose by profile: split the race into run time, station time, roxzone time → the biggest addressable loss is the target.
  • Strong-but-slow → aerobic ceiling is the usual limiter; ↑ easy-run volume + threshold work, hold strength at maintenance (1–2 quality sessions/wk with intensity kept high) to limit detraining.
  • Runner-fails-on-sleds → force deficit. Sleds are high-force efforts against friction with a large maximal-strength contribution — they are also metabolically brutal, so this is a strength emphasis, not a claim that sleds are 'not cardio'. ↑ heavy squat/leg press/hip-hinge + prowler intervals at or near race load.
  • Sled push/pull cost scales strongly with friction (floor surface, sled runners, venue) — coach technique (low body angle, short choppy steps, avoid stopping on push; hip-hinge and rhythmic arm-over-arm on pull) before adding load, since each restart means overcoming static friction again.
  • Recurring calf issues → most often a capacity problem; progressive calf loading (straight- and bent-knee, heavy and slow), sane run-volume progression, and review eccentric exposure from lunges and wall balls. Rule out non-training drivers (footwear change, prior injury, sleep/fuelling) before assuming pure load error.
  • Compromised running (running under station fatigue) is a specific trainable quality → run→station→run couplets, not isolated sessions.
  • First race peak → roughly 1–2 week taper, ↓ volume ~40–60% with intensity largely retained (the pattern best supported in the taper literature); rehearse pacing, transitions, wall-ball sets, and fuelling. Meaningful fitness gains in the final two weeks are unlikely, so execution is the lever.
  • Wall balls are 100 reps in every individual division — only ball weight and target height change (Open men 6 kg to a 3.00 m target, Open women 4 kg to 2.70 m; Pro men 9 kg to 3.00 m, Pro women 6 kg to 2.70 m). This is the classic blow-up point. Pre-plan set sizes (e.g. 25/25/20/15/15) rather than going to failure.
  • Pacing: the first 1 km should feel restrained — going out hard costs disproportionately in runs 5–8; roxzone discipline is comparatively free speed for most amateurs, with no change in fitness required.
  • Re-test the same limiter after 4–8 weeks; if the metric didn't move, revisit the diagnosis before repeating or intensifying the block.

Running with pre-fatigued legs and elevated ventilation immediately after a station. Trained as a quality distinct from fresh running.

The transition area between run and station. Time spent there counts toward total race time and is often the cheapest performance gain.
Strength-endurance
The capacity to repeat submaximal force output with minimal decay — a dominant quality on sleds, carries, lunges and wall balls.
Load tolerance
How much cumulative mechanical stress a tissue can absorb before symptoms. Raised primarily by progressive loading rather than by rest or stretching alone.
Maintenance dosing
The minimum training frequency/volume that preserves an adaptation while resources shift elsewhere — often 1–2 sessions per week, with intensity preserved even as volume drops.
Taper
A planned pre-race reduction in volume with intensity largely retained, to shed fatigue while preserving fitness.
Needs analysis
The structured comparison of race demands against the athlete's current profile, producing a ranked list of gaps to train.

Second half of the race slower than the first — often evidence of a pacing error, though it can also reflect a genuine endurance ceiling.
Connection to your training

When an athlete brings you a race result, don't prescribe from feel — split their time into runs, stations, and roxzone, and compare each against their peer group to find the likely limiter. If their sleds are the outlier, spend a block on heavy push/pull strength rather than adding more intervals; if their late runs collapse, look first at pacing and compromised-run couplets. Re-test the same segment in about six weeks and let the number, rather than the athlete's preference, decide whether you keep the lever or change it.

Multi-race & in-season management

In-season HYROX planning replaces linear build logic with a repeating reload → mini-build → taper → race loop. A race is a maximal effort lasting roughly 55–60 min for elites and commonly 90 min to over 2 h for age-group athletes: 8 km of running split around eight stations, with the eccentric-damaging elements (sandbag lunges, wall-ball catches, burpee broad-jump landings, and the running itself) sitting alongside high-tension but concentric-dominant sled work. That damage outlasts metabolic and autonomic recovery, so readiness markers return before tissue does. The coach's job is to decide, race by race, whether the gap allows a genuine rebuild (roughly 4+ weeks), only maintenance (roughly 2–3 weeks), or pure recovery plus taper (under 2 weeks). Maintenance works because intensity preserves adaptation while volume can drop sharply. Cumulative fatigue across a block is managed by ranking races A/B/C and defending only the A races with full taper and full reload.

Key facts to memorize
  • Race block = repeating loop: reload → mini-build → taper → race, not one long linear plan.
  • Rank races A/B/C. Only A races get a full taper + full post-race reload; B/C races are trained through.
  • Typical inter-race gaps: <2 wk → recover + taper only; 2–3 wk → maintenance + short sharpening; ≥4 wk → real rebuild block possible. These are planning heuristics, not fixed thresholds — individual recovery varies widely.
  • Maintenance rule: hold intensity, cut volume (commonly ~1/3 to ~2/3) and keep frequency roughly intact → strength and VO2max are largely retained for several weeks.
  • Rough detraining order: plasma volume falls first (within days); anaerobic/repeat-effort capacity over ~1–3 wk; VO2max meaningfully declines from ~2–4 wk; maximal strength is the most durable (largely held for several weeks, though power and rate of force development fade sooner).
  • Muscle-damage markers (soreness, ↓ eccentric force, ↓ jump height) recover more slowly than HR/HRV → don't restart heavy lunge, wall-ball and running volume on HRV alone.
  • The eccentric-damaging race elements are sandbag lunges, wall-ball catches, burpee broad-jump landings and the 8 km of running. Sled push and pull are concentric-dominant — very high tension and locally fatiguing, but they are not a major source of eccentric muscle damage.
  • Post-race reload: 2–4 easy days (Z1–Z2, mobility), then easy aerobic running, then compromised running, and only then heavy eccentric station volume.
  • Cumulative-fatigue red flags: ↑ RHR, ↓ HRV trend, ↓ wall-ball/burpee capacity at same RPE, rising roxzone times, sleep + mood decline.
  • When rebuilding mid-season, raise volume gradually — ~10%/week is a common coaching rule of thumb rather than a validated injury threshold, but abrupt post-race spikes onto damaged tissue are widely implicated in in-season injury.
  • Recovery between races is slower in older athletes and in those with less training history — extend reloads rather than compressing them.
  • Skill/station technique work is cheap to maintain — low volume, low fatigue → keep it in even the tightest weeks.

A stretch of the calendar containing two or more races where full training progression is subordinated to race readiness. Load is shaped around race dates rather than a single peak.

A priority ranking for events. A races get a full taper and full recovery; B races get a short taper; C races are treated as hard training or pacing rehearsals.

Reduced-volume, intensity-preserving training that holds an existing adaptation rather than building it. Enough to stop decay, cheap enough to allow recovery.
Reload

A short planned overload that temporarily depresses performance (days to weeks) but supercompensates after adequate recovery. It becomes non-functional overreaching — a longer decline needing weeks to months — if the recovery window is skipped.
Sharpening
A brief phase of race-specific, race-pace work — compromised running, station transitions, roxzone drills — with low overall volume, used when there is no time to rebuild.
Residual eccentric damage (informal shorthand: 'eccentric load debt')
Not a formal textbook term — a working label for muscle damage from the high-eccentric race elements (sandbag lunges, wall-ball catches, jump landings, running) that lingers after metabolic and autonomic markers have normalised.
Roxzone
The HYROX transition area between each run and its station. Cumulative roxzone time is a sensitive in-season fatigue marker because it degrades before pace does.
Connection to your training

Map your athlete's whole season on one page first, mark one or two A races, then work backwards to decide which gaps allow a rebuild and which only allow maintenance. If two races sit three weeks apart, give days 1–4 as reload, days 5–14 as one hard compromised-run session plus one heavy strength/sled session per week at reduced volume, then a 5–7 day taper — do not chase new strength PRs in that window. Reintroduce lunge and wall-ball volume later than sled work, since those carry the eccentric damage. Track wall-ball capacity and roxzone times across the block; when those drift up at the same RPE, cut a race or convert it to a C-priority pacing rehearsal.

Practice design — blocked, random & variable

How you schedule practice matters as much as how much you do. Blocked practice (AAA BBB) builds fast within-session gains but weaker retention; random/interleaved practice (ABC BCA) feels worse in-session yet typically produces better retention and transfer — the contextual interference effect. Its mechanism is still debated: the two long-standing explanations are that interleaving forces the motor plan to be forgotten and reconstructed each trial, and that it invites richer inter-task comparison and elaboration. The effect is not universal — it is most reliable in relatively skilled learners on moderately complex tasks, and can shrink or reverse in true novices or on very complex skills, so match the difficulty of the schedule to the athlete's current competence. Constant practice fixes one parameter set; variable practice samples loads, speeds and grips, widening the generalised motor programme so novel race conditions are covered. Part practice suits low-organisation, high-complexity skills; whole practice suits high-organisation continuous skills. For HYROX, transfer demands practising stations pre-fatigued, in race order, with race-standard cues.

Key facts to memorize
  • Blocked = high acquisition, lower retention; random = depressed acquisition, better retention + transfer (contextual interference).
  • The contextual interference mechanism is contested — forgetting-and-reconstruction and elaboration/inter-task comparison are competing explanations, not settled fact.
  • Contextual interference is conditional, not automatic: strongest for competent learners on moderately complex tasks, weak or reversed in novices and on very complex skills — scale schedule difficulty to the athlete.
  • Performance during practice ≠ learning — judge by delayed retention/transfer tests, not session-day scores.
  • Learning progression: blocked → serial → random as competence rises; novices need some blocked exposure first.
  • Constant practice = one parameter (fixed load/pace); variable practice = many parameters → broader motor schema.
  • Vary the parameters that genuinely differ between venues and race days (sled load, flooring friction under the sled, sandbag shape and placement, rope thickness, footwear/surface), not the movement standard itself — standards such as wall-ball target height are fixed by division and must be trained at spec, never varied.
  • Part practice fits low-organisation/high-complexity skills (sled pull hand-over-hand); whole practice fits continuous high-organisation ones (SkiErg, row, run).
  • Progressive part / whole-part-whole beats isolated drilling that never rejoins the full skill.
  • Specificity: transfer ↑ when practice matches race context — fatigue state, roxzone entry, footwear, surface, noise.
  • Fatigued practice must be technique-gated: stop the set when the movement standard breaks, or you groove the fault.
  • Random ≠ chaotic — session order is planned; only the athlete's expectancy is randomised. Short blocked blocks still repair a named fault.
Visual glossary
Contextual interference
The learning benefit that arises when practice trials are interleaved rather than repeated in blocks. It typically slows acquisition but improves long-term retention and transfer, most reliably in learners who already have a workable movement pattern.
Blocked practice
All repetitions of one task completed before moving to the next. Produces rapid, often misleading within-session improvement.
Random (interleaved) practice
Tasks rotated unpredictably so the athlete must rebuild the motor plan each trial. Lower session performance, better retention.
Variable practice
Repeating one skill class while varying its parameters — load, tempo, distance, implement — to widen the generalised motor programme.
Generalised motor programme
A stored movement pattern whose invariant structure (order of elements, relative timing, relative force) is fixed but whose parameters (overall force, overall duration, amplitude) are scaled to the demand at hand.
Part practice
Breaking a skill into components trained separately, then reintegrated. Best for skills with distinct, weakly interdependent parts.
Whole practice
Rehearsing the skill in full. Preferred for continuous, highly organised actions where segmenting destroys the rhythm.
Transfer
The degree to which practised capability carries into a different context — for HYROX, into deep fatigue on competition equipment.
Retention test
A delayed re-test after a rest interval, used to measure learning rather than temporary practice-day performance.
Connection to your training

Stop letting your athletes drill wall balls fresh in tidy sets of 20 — that is blocked, constant, unfatigued practice, and it flatters them. Once the standard is clean, interleave: 400 m run → 15 wall balls → 100 m sandbag lunges → 200 m farmers carry, rotated in an order they cannot predict, with sled loads varied around race spec and the wall-ball target held at division height. Keep short blocked corrections in the warm-up for one named fault, then hand the skill back to a fatigued, race-ordered environment and cut the set the moment depth or catch position degrades. For a genuine novice, resist the urge to interleave immediately — build a repeatable pattern in blocks first, or the added schedule difficulty just buys you worse reps.

Which HYROX elements carry the real eccentric damage, and why is the sled a common misattribution?
How sure are you?
Answer
Sandbag lunges, wall-ball catches, burpee broad-jump landings and the 8 km of running are the eccentric loads. Sled push and pull are concentric-dominant — extremely high tension and locally fatiguing, but they generate little eccentric muscle damage, which is why sleds are often used as low-damage conditioning.
