Routines, journaling & reflective practice

Routines and journaling are the two habits that make everything else in mental performance durable. A routine is a fixed sequence of actions that automates behaviour when working memory is compromised — pre-race, pre-station, and post-error. Its value is not superstition but load reduction: the athlete does not have to decide anything, so attention stays on the task. Good routines are short, portable, controllable and rehearsed; anything dependent on a specific location, object or condition becomes a liability when a race disrupts it. Journaling serves the parallel function for learning. Sport psychology only compounds if the athlete notices patterns across weeks, and memory for subjective states is poor and biased by recent results. A structured log — what was done, how it felt, what was noticed, what to change — turns scattered experiences into a data set the athlete and coach can act on. The recurring reflective prompt is deliberately simple: three things learned, three things already done well, three things to improve.

Key facts to memorize
  • A routine automates behaviour so attention and degraded working memory are not spent on decisions during high arousal.
  • Three routine types worth building: pre-race (the hours and minutes before the start), pre-station or pre-effort (seconds before a repeatable task), and post-error reset.
  • Good routines are short, portable, fully controllable and rehearsed. Routines dependent on a specific object, place or condition break when the race disrupts them.
  • Distinguish routine from superstition: a routine has a functional mechanism (attention, arousal, sequencing); a superstition attributes outcome to an unrelated act and creates anxiety when broken.
  • A pre-station routine can be as small as a breath, a grip check and a cue word — small enough to survive fatigue.
  • The post-error reset is the highest-value routine in HYROX, where a bad station otherwise contaminates the following run.
  • Rehearse routines in training under fatigue. A routine used only on race day is not a routine.
  • Journaling counters the unreliability of memory for subjective states, which is heavily biased by the most recent session or result.
  • A useful log captures both objective (session, splits, load) and subjective (RPE, mood, sleep, stress, what was noticed) data — the interaction is where the insight sits.
  • The three-by-three reflective prompt — three things learned, three already done well, three to improve — is simple enough to sustain and forces balanced reflection rather than pure fault-finding.
  • Reflection-in-action (adjusting during the session) and reflection-on-action (reviewing afterwards) are complementary; journaling develops the second and gradually improves the first.
  • Patterns only emerge across weeks: recurring pre-race sleep disruption, a station that always precedes a mood drop, a taper that consistently feels flat.
  • Coaches should journal too. Reflective practice is a coach-development tool as much as an athlete one, and modelling it raises athlete adherence.
Visual glossary
Pre-performance routine
A fixed, rehearsed sequence of actions before a performance that automates behaviour and preserves attention when arousal degrades working memory.
Post-error reset
A short, deliberate routine executed immediately after a mistake to return attention to the task and prevent contamination of the next effort.
Superstition
An act believed to influence outcome without a functional mechanism. Unlike a routine, it generates anxiety when it cannot be performed.
Reflection-in-action
Noticing and adjusting during performance itself, as distinct from reviewing afterwards.
Reflection-on-action
Structured review after the event, converting experience into transferable learning. The habit journaling is designed to build.
Three-by-three review
A reflective prompt capturing three things learned, three already done well and three to improve — simple enough to sustain and balanced by design.
Connection to your training

Give every athlete three routines and make them rehearse all three in training: what they do in the last twenty minutes before a start, what they do in the five seconds before a station, and what they do immediately after something goes wrong. Keep them short and portable — a breath, a cue word, eyes up — because anything elaborate will break on race day. Then get them writing. A few lines after each session covering what they did, how it felt, and what they noticed, plus the three-by-three review at the end of each week: three things learned, three things already going well, three to improve. Read it. The patterns you spot across a month — the station that always precedes a bad run, the week they always feel flat — are things neither of you will remember accurately without the log.

Training the gut

Gastrointestinal distress is one of the most common reasons endurance athletes underperform, and it is substantially trainable. The gut adapts to what it is regularly asked to do: repeated exposure to carbohydrate during exercise upregulates intestinal transporters, increases gastric emptying rate and improves tolerance, so an athlete who never practises fuelling cannot suddenly absorb race-day carbohydrate. The mechanism sits alongside the exercise-induced splanchnic blood flow reduction — blood is diverted to working muscle and skin, reducing gut perfusion and impairing absorption, an effect worsened by high intensity, heat and dehydration. Practically, gut training means progressively increasing carbohydrate intake during sessions over weeks, rehearsing the exact race-day products, and using multiple transportable carbohydrates (glucose plus fructose) to exceed the roughly 60 g/h ceiling imposed by a single transporter pathway. HYROX adds a specific complication: bouncing, bracing, wall balls and lunges mechanically agitate the gut in a way steady running does not.

Key facts to memorize
  • The gut is adaptable: regular carbohydrate exposure during exercise upregulates intestinal transporters, speeds gastric emptying and improves tolerance.
  • Glucose absorption via SGLT1 saturates at roughly 60 g/h; adding fructose, absorbed via GLUT5, allows total intakes around 90 g/h or higher in well-trained guts.
  • Typical multiple-transportable-carbohydrate ratios are around 2:1 glucose:fructose, with some newer products using 1:0.8.
  • Splanchnic hypoperfusion — reduced gut blood flow as blood is diverted to muscle and skin — impairs absorption and is worsened by high intensity, heat and dehydration.
  • Dehydration markedly worsens GI symptoms; fluid strategy and fuelling strategy cannot be planned separately.
  • Train the gut progressively across weeks, raising intake per hour in steps rather than jumping to race targets.
  • Rehearse with the exact race-day products, concentrations and timing. Novel products on race day are a common and avoidable failure.
  • High-fibre, high-fat and high-protein intakes close to competition slow gastric emptying and raise symptom risk; lower-residue choices in the final 24 hours help symptom-prone athletes.
  • Symptoms split into upper (nausea, reflux, fullness) and lower (cramping, urgency, diarrhoea), with somewhat different drivers and fixes.
  • HYROX-specific mechanical agitation — jumping, bracing under the sandbag, repeated squat-to-overhead — provokes symptoms that steady-state running does not, so rehearse fuelling in station work, not just on runs.
  • Very high carbohydrate concentrations and hypertonic drinks slow gastric emptying; dilute appropriately.
  • Some athletes are genuinely low absorbers and will not reach 90 g/h. Individualise to the tested tolerance rather than a published target.
  • Persistent or severe symptoms, blood in stool, or symptoms outside exercise warrant medical referral rather than further gut training.
Visual glossary
Gut training
Progressive, repeated exposure to carbohydrate and fluid during exercise, upregulating absorption capacity and improving tolerance over weeks.
Multiple transportable carbohydrates
Combining glucose and fructose to use separate intestinal transporters, allowing oxidation rates above the ~60 g/h single-pathway ceiling.
SGLT1 / GLUT5
The intestinal transporters for glucose and fructose respectively. Their separate saturation points are why mixed-carbohydrate products work.
Splanchnic hypoperfusion
Reduced blood flow to the gut during exercise as blood is redirected to muscle and skin, impairing absorption and driving GI symptoms.
Gastric emptying
The rate at which stomach contents pass to the small intestine. Slowed by high intensity, dehydration, and hypertonic or fat- and fibre-rich intake.
Low-residue diet
Reduced-fibre eating in the final 24 hours before competition, used to lower gut contents and symptom risk in susceptible athletes.
Connection to your training

If your athlete's stomach is the reason their race falls apart, treat it as a trainable system and put it in the plan. Start below their current tolerance and add carbohydrate per hour in steps over six to eight weeks, using the exact gel or drink they'll race with — not a similar one. Use a glucose-fructose product if you're going past about 60 g an hour, since that's where the single transporter tops out. Practise it during station work as well as running, because bracing under a sandbag and throwing wall balls upsets a full stomach in a way a steady jog never will. Get the hydration right at the same time, since dehydration makes everything worse. And if symptoms are severe, persistent or happening outside training, that's a referral.

Annual plan & race calendar

An annual plan is built backwards from one dated A-race. Fix the race, count back the weeks, then fill the calendar with phases: preparation (general → specific), competition (peak, taper, race), transition (unloading, restoration). General prep builds the raw material — aerobic base, strength, tissue tolerance, technique on all eight stations. Specific prep converts it, compressing work density and adding compromised running so the athlete rehearses race physiology, not just its parts. Volume generally falls as specificity and intensity rise. B and C races are training stimuli and dress rehearsals, deliberately not tapered for. Sequencing is the variable coaches most often get wrong: each phase must leave the athlete able to absorb the next, and a well-ordered plan at moderate intensity will usually beat a harder but incoherent one.

Key facts to memorize
  • Plan backwards: fix A-race date → count weeks → assign phases → then write sessions.
  • Phases: preparation (general + specific) → competition (peak/taper/race) → transition.
  • A race = full taper + peak, 1–2 per year; B = tested but trained through; C = practice/skill exposure, no unload.
  • Race-specific block to a target race is typically 16–24 weeks; 8–12 only for an already-trained athlete.
  • Fixed HYROX order (8x1km runs alternating with 8 stations): SkiErg → Sled Push → Sled Pull → Burpee Broad Jump → Row → Farmers Carry → Sandbag Lunges → Wall Balls. The order is fixed, so plan position dictates fatigue state.
  • General prep ↑ volume, ↓ intensity, wide movement base; specific prep ↓ volume, ↑ race-pace + compromised work.
  • Compromised (run→station→run) training introduced meaningfully ~8–12 wk out; sharpened in final 4–6 wk.
  • Full-distance/near-full simulations: sparingly, roughly every 3–4 wk in specific prep, last one ≥2 wk pre-race.
  • Taper ~7–14 days: volume ↓ roughly 40–60%, intensity and race-pace touches retained, frequency held. These are common ranges from taper research, not a universal prescription — individual responses vary.
  • Transition 1–3 wk after A-race: unstructured, low load — restores tissue + motivation, not detraining.
  • Loading pattern commonly 2–3 build weeks : 1 deload; deload = ↓ volume first, keep some intensity. The ratio is a practical convention, not a validated constant.
  • Race-order specificity matters: wall balls and sandbag lunges are trained pre-fatigued, not fresh.
Visual glossary
Macrocycle
The largest planning unit. In classical (NSCA/Bompa) terminology this is usually the full training year; in common race-prep usage it is loosely applied to the whole multi-month block leading to one target race. Either way it contains the full phase sequence from general preparation through transition, and is subdivided into mesocycles (typically ~2–6 wk) and microcycles (typically ~1 wk).
A/B/C race
Priority tiers. A = the peaked goal race; B = competitive but trained through with minimal unload; C = low-stakes exposure used as a hard training day or skill rehearsal.
General preparation
Early phase building broad capacities — aerobic base, strength, work tolerance, technique — with limited resemblance to race demands.
Specific preparation
Later phase where sessions increasingly mirror race order, pace, load and fatigue state, converting general fitness into race performance.
Compromised training
Running performed with pre-existing station fatigue (or stations performed after running), rehearsing the neuromuscular and metabolic state of the actual race.
Taper
Planned reduction in training load before an A-race, cutting volume while largely preserving intensity and frequency, to shed accumulated fatigue while retaining fitness.
Transition phase
Deliberate low-load period after a peak race for physical and psychological restoration before the next macrocycle begins.
Roxzone
The transition area an athlete crosses between the running lane and the station. In HYROX results, roxzone time is reported as the total accumulated transition time across the whole race — every run→station and station→run movement, not a single crossing. It is a trainable skill (approach, setup, exit) and commonly accounts for several minutes of finish time.
Backwards planning (note: not 'reverse periodization')
Constructing the calendar from the race date backwards so every week has a defined purpose relative to the peak. Do not confuse this with reverse periodization, which is a distinct and unrelated concept describing a model that begins with low-volume/high-intensity work and progresses toward higher volume — the opposite of the traditional model.
Connection to your training

Start every athlete's plan by writing the A-race date at the right-hand end of a week-by-week grid and filling leftward — you will immediately see whether you have 20 weeks or 9, and the honest answer changes what you can build. Hold general strength and aerobic base work early; do not let an athlete's enthusiasm for full simulations pull race-specific compromised work forward past ~12 weeks out, or they risk arriving flat. Register B and C races on the same grid and tell the athlete explicitly which ones they are not tapering for, so a mid-block result is never read as a failure.

Load management & managing niggles

Load management is keeping applied load under tissue capacity while still driving adaptation. Most HYROX breakdowns are not accidents — they are accumulated: 8 km of fatigued running plus burpee broad jumps, sandbag lunges and wall balls loads the same lower limb repeatedly. The coach's job is triage, not diagnosis. Classify what the athlete reports as soreness (diffuse, symmetrical, eases with warm-up, settling over a few days), a niggle (local, reproducible, low-grade, no loss of quality) or an injury (sharp, night/rest pain, swelling, altered mechanics, function lost). Then apply traffic-light rules: modify the stimulus, keep the intent, substitute off-feet, and progress running volume gradually rather than stopping everything.

Key facts to memorize
  • DOMS: onset ~12–24 h, peaks ~24–72 h, and usually resolves within 5–7 days; unaccustomed eccentric-heavy work (lunges, downhill running, burpee broad jump landings) drives it most. Soreness still worsening after a week is a flag to reassess, not to push.
  • Soreness = diffuse + bilateral + eases with warm-up. Niggle = focal + reproducible on a specific movement. Injury = sharp/night pain, swelling, giving way, or gait change.
  • 24-hour response rule: symptoms may rise during a session but should return to baseline by the next morning, or the load was too high.
  • Pain-monitoring model (Silbernagel et al., 2007, developed for Achilles tendinopathy and since applied more broadly to tendon rehab): pain up to ~5/10 during activity is acceptable provided it does not keep climbing week to week and the morning-after score returns toward baseline. It is a tendon-loading guide, not a rule for bone or acute injury.
  • Traffic-light bands used in this lesson are deliberately more conservative in-session than the tendon model: GREEN (0–2/10, no function loss) → train as planned, monitor. AMBER (3–5/10, technique intact) → modify volume/intensity/range/impact, keep the session intent. RED (>5/10, limp, swelling, night pain, focal bone pain) → stop the aggravating stimulus, refer.
  • Focal, point-tender bone pain that worsens with each impact and hurts on single-leg hopping = suspected bone stress injury → RED, no traffic-light trial, medical referral.
  • Off-feet substitutions preserve aerobic dose with ↓ ground-reaction load: SkiErg, Row, bike/AirBike, pool run, incline walk (reduced-impact rather than truly zero-impact). Match duration + intensity zone, not distance.
  • Eccentric demand is not evenly spread across the stations. Highest: sandbag lunges, burpee broad jump landings, wall ball catch/descent, and long or compromised running. Lowest: sled push and sled pull, which are near-purely concentric — they are often the lower-body work you can keep in a modified week rather than the first thing to cut.
  • A common rule of thumb is to progress weekly running volume by roughly 5–10 % with a down-week every 3–4 weeks. Note the '10 % rule' is a convention, not a well-evidenced threshold — trials have not shown it reduces injury — so treat it as a starting point and let symptoms, training age and history set the real number.
  • Acute:chronic workload ratio ~0.8–1.3 is the commonly cited 'sweet spot'; ratios ≳1.5 flag a spike. Treat as a rough monitoring heuristic, not a law — the model has been substantially criticised on methodological grounds.
  • Modify one variable at a time (volume → intensity → impact → range) so you can tell what actually helped; never take the session away without giving something back.
Visual glossary
Tissue capacity
The load a structure can currently absorb and recover from without symptoms. It rises with consistent, progressive exposure and falls with detraining, illness or poor sleep.
Niggle
A low-grade, localised, reproducible complaint that does not yet limit performance or alter technique. It is a warning signal, not an injury — the window where modification still works.
DOMS
Delayed-onset muscle soreness: diffuse muscular discomfort appearing within a day of unaccustomed or eccentric-heavy work, peaking around 24–72 h, easing with warm-up and typically clearing inside a week.
24-hour response rule
A monitoring standard where a session is judged acceptable if any symptom provoked returns to its pre-session baseline by the next morning.
Traffic-light system
A green/amber/red decision framework that converts an athlete's reported symptoms into a preset training action, so decisions are consistent rather than mood-driven.
Off-feet work
Aerobic training with little or no ground impact — erg, bike, pool, incline walk — used to keep the aerobic stimulus while unloading bone and tendon.
Acute:chronic workload ratio (ACWR)
Short-term load (typically a 7-day acute window) divided by the rolling average of recent weeks (typically 28 days). Used as a rough flag for load spikes rather than a validated injury predictor.
Compromised running
Running performed under pre-existing station fatigue, as in HYROX. It carries different mechanical and metabolic cost than fresh running and must be progressed as its own load.
Deload
A planned reduction in volume — with intensity usually held rather than cut — for a week or so, allowing accumulated fatigue to dissipate and adaptation to surface.
Connection to your training

When an athlete messages you 'my shin's been a bit sore since the race sim', don't guess — ask where exactly, what it scores out of 10, whether it hurts in the morning, and whether it changes their gait. If it's diffuse calf soreness at 2/10 you keep the session; if it's a pinpoint spot on the tibia that worsens with each stride, you stop running that week and rebuild the aerobic dose on the SkiErg, row and bike at the same durations and zones — and refer, because focal bone pain is outside a coach's scope to manage. Keep the strength work and the upper-body stations training as normal, then reintroduce running as short, frequent, easy blocks before touching compromised running or burpee broad jumps again.

Compromised running — the physiology

Compromised running is running on legs and lungs already loaded by station work. Economy degrades mainly for peripheral reasons: the muscles just taxed arrive at the run with depleted phosphocreatine, accumulated H+ and inorganic phosphate, and impaired excitation-contraction coupling, so more motor units — including higher-threshold type II units — are recruited to hold the same pace, and oxygen cost per metre rises. Central drive is simultaneously reduced by afferent feedback from fatigued muscle. Perfusion is still partly committed to recently working limbs, to skin for heat loss and to hard-working respiratory muscles, so the legs are competing for a finite cardiac output. Mechanics change too: stride shortens, ground-contact time lengthens, hip extension and leg stiffness fall, elastic return is poorer. Same pace now costs higher HR, VO2 and RPE.

Key facts to memorize
  • Running economy = O2 (or energy) cost at a fixed submaximal speed; post-station it ↑ (worse) even when pace is unchanged.
  • Peripheral fatigue dominates early post-station: PCr depletion, ↑H+/Pi, impaired Ca2+ release and reduced myofibrillar Ca2+ sensitivity → force per active fibre ↓ → extra motor-unit recruitment → ↑VO2 at the same speed.
  • Central fatigue = ↓voluntary drive to the motoneuron pool, driven partly by group III/IV afferent feedback from fatigued muscle. The same afferent traffic raises perceived effort, which is a large part of why athletes settle out of the roxzone rather than surge.
  • Blood-flow competition: a finite cardiac output is shared between recently loaded muscle (post-exercise hyperaemia), skin for heat dissipation, and the respiratory muscles — so O2 delivery per unit of locomotor muscle falls.
  • Note the direction of each reflex: the LOCOMOTOR muscle metaboreflex raises blood pressure and sympathetic tone to defend flow TO the working muscle. It is the RESPIRATORY muscle metaboreflex that redistributes flow AWAY from the legs when ventilatory work is high. Do not conflate them.
  • Heavy ventilatory work after SkiErg and Row is the classic HYROX trigger for the respiratory metaboreflex. Mechanistically this is well established in laboratory loading studies; its exact size during a real race has not been quantified, so treat it as a plausible contributor, not a measured one.
  • Mechanical signature of the compromised run — robust findings: stride length ↓, ground-contact time ↑, hip extension ↓, leg/ankle stiffness ↓, braking impulse ↑. Cadence usually holds or rises slightly relative to the shortened stride. Vertical oscillation is inconsistent across studies — do not teach it as a reliable marker.
  • Station-specific carry-over: sled push and sled pull are near-purely concentric, high-force, heavily glycolytic work that hammers quads and glutes; sandbag lunges pre-fatigue the same hip and knee extensors used for propulsion; ski, row and farmers carry load posture, grip and trunk → early-run postural collapse.
  • HR drifts up and stays elevated across the 8 runs while pace ↓ — cardiovascular drift plus rising RPE, not the arrival of a new aerobic limit.
  • The first couple of hundred metres out of the roxzone is the worst window; economy partially recovers as PCr resynthesises (fast-component half-time commonly reported around 20-30 s, with several minutes needed for full restoration) — but only if pace is controlled rather than surged.
  • Race-order fact worth memorising for transfer questions: the 8 stations run SkiErg, Sled Push, Sled Pull, Burpee Broad Jump, Row, Farmers Carry, Sandbag Lunges, Wall Balls, each preceded by a 1 km run. Wall balls is LAST — there is no run after it, so wall-ball fatigue cannot compromise a subsequent run. Run 8 is the one that follows sandbag lunges.
  • Best trainable adaptations: aerobic/mitochondrial base for faster metabolite clearance and PCr resynthesis, muscle buffering and lactate transport capacity, strength-endurance in the exact station patterns, and repeated station→run transitions so pacing and mechanics are rehearsed under fatigue.
Visual glossary
Compromised running
Running performed immediately after a fatiguing non-running task, so pace must be produced by muscles already partially depleted and by a partly suppressed central drive.
Running economy
The oxygen or energy cost of covering a set distance at a set submaximal speed. Lower cost = better economy; fatigue typically worsens it.
Peripheral fatigue
Loss of force-generating capacity arising at or distal to the neuromuscular junction — substrate depletion, metabolite accumulation and disrupted calcium handling inside the fibre.
Central fatigue
A fatigue-related reduction in neural drive from the brain and spinal cord to the working muscle, reducing voluntary activation independently of the contractile state of the muscle.
Group III/IV afferents
Sensory nerves in muscle that report mechanical and metabolic stress to the CNS; their firing raises perceived effort, inhibits central drive and triggers cardiovascular reflexes.
Locomotor muscle metaboreflex
A pressor reflex triggered by metabolite build-up in working locomotor muscle. It raises blood pressure and sympathetic outflow, which acts to preserve perfusion of that muscle.
Respiratory muscle metaboreflex
The equivalent reflex arising from fatiguing respiratory muscles. Because it drives sympathetic vasoconstriction in the limbs, it shifts blood flow toward the breathing muscles and away from the legs — the version that matters most for a post-SkiErg or post-Row run.
Roxzone
The HYROX transition area an athlete crosses between the running track and the station area; the window in which reacquiring efficient running mechanics is hardest.
Strength-endurance
The capacity to repeat sub-maximal high-force contractions with minimal force decay — the quality that keeps sled, lunge and carry work from wrecking the following run.
Cardiovascular drift
A progressive rise in heart rate at a constant workload, accompanied by falling stroke volume and rising RPE, driven by heat strain, plasma-volume loss and rising sympathetic tone.
Connection to your training

Stop programming runs and stations as fully separate sessions. For most HYROX athletes the binding limiter is the run immediately out of the roxzone rather than standalone 1 km speed — confirm that for the individual athlete before you reorganise their whole block, because a genuinely slow runner still needs run speed. Build blocks like 50 m sled push → 1 km at target race pace, repeated 4-6 times, and coach the pattern they will actually need out of the zone: quick turnover on a deliberately short stride, tall posture, then lengthen the stride as the legs come back. Track the gap between fresh 1 km pace and compromised 1 km pace. Shrinking that gap is the adaptation this session targets; raising fresh pace is a separate and still-necessary job.

The doubles athlete

Doubles is a genuinely different event, not a halved single race. Both partners run every metre together, but station work is shared — so the total running volume is unchanged while station volume per athlete roughly halves, and the intensity at which stations are performed rises sharply because each athlete works in bursts with recovery. That changes the physiological profile: doubles rewards repeated high-intensity efforts and fast recovery between them more than steady strength-endurance, and it rewards pace-matching in the runs. The coaching problem becomes pairing and strategy as much as conditioning. Partners must be matched closely enough on running pace that neither is racing at the wrong intensity for the full 8 km, while their station strengths ideally complement each other so the work split can be asymmetric where it pays. Communication, changeover mechanics and a pre-agreed contingency plan for when one partner degrades are the difference between a good pair and two good individuals racing badly together.

Key facts to memorize
  • In doubles both athletes complete all 8 km of running together; only the station work is divided. Running volume per athlete is unchanged from the individual race.
  • Because station work is shared, each athlete performs roughly half the station volume — but at a higher intensity, with partial recovery between bursts.
  • The physiological emphasis shifts toward repeated high-intensity work capacity and recovery between efforts, rather than sustained strength-endurance at a moderate intensity.
  • Running pace compatibility is the single biggest pairing criterion: the pair runs at the slower partner's sustainable pace, and a mismatched faster partner spends the race under-working while the slower one runs over threshold.
  • Complementary station strengths allow an asymmetric split — give a partner more of the station they are strong at rather than defaulting to 50/50 everywhere.
  • Changeover cost is real. Every swap has a transition cost, so fewer, larger blocks are usually faster than many small alternations, unless fatigue forces the split.
  • Agree the split per station in advance, including the fallback if someone is struggling. Improvising splits mid-race under hypoxic stress produces poor decisions.
  • Wall balls at the end are where pairs most often unravel — plan the break pattern and the swap trigger (a rep count, not a feeling) before the race.
  • Sled push and pull are usually where a strength mismatch pays most: assign the stronger partner a larger share, since sled time is highly sensitive to absolute strength.
  • Train together at least some of the time. Changeover mechanics, communication under load and shared pacing are skills, and they degrade under fatigue like any other.
  • Communication should be pre-agreed and minimal — short, unambiguous calls, because complex verbal exchange at race intensity fails.
  • Doubles pacing errors usually come from the early runs: the pair feels fresh because station work was shared and goes out too hard, then pays over the back half.
  • The psychological dimension cuts both ways — accountability to a partner raises effort, but guilt at holding a partner back can push an athlete past sustainable intensity. Manage it explicitly.
Visual glossary
Work split
The pre-agreed division of station repetitions or distance between doubles partners, ideally asymmetric to exploit complementary strengths.
Changeover
The transition when doubles partners swap on a station. Each swap carries a time cost, so the number of swaps is itself a strategic variable.
Pace compatibility
How closely two partners' sustainable running paces match. The pair necessarily runs at the slower athlete's sustainable pace, making this the primary pairing criterion.
Swap trigger
An objective, pre-agreed cue — usually a rep count or distance — that dictates when partners change over, replacing in-race judgement under fatigue.
Repeated high-intensity capacity
The ability to produce and repeat near-maximal efforts with partial recovery between them. The dominant station quality in doubles racing.
Asymmetric split
Allocating a larger share of a station to the partner better suited to it, rather than dividing every station equally.
Connection to your training

Pair on running pace first and station strengths second — if their sustainable 1 km pace differs by much, no amount of clever station splitting will fix the race. Once paired, write the split station by station before race week, giving the stronger partner more of the sled and pre-agreeing the wall-ball break pattern with a rep-count swap trigger rather than 'when I'm cooked'. Train the changeovers, because the transition is a skill and a sloppy swap costs seconds every station. Programme for the actual demand: shorter, harder station intervals with incomplete recovery, run at a shared pace, rather than the long grinding station sets a solo athlete needs. And rehearse the contingency — what happens when one of them blows up — so the answer exists before they need it.

How would you structure one key compromised-running session six weeks out?

Answer
Race-order pairs: 5-6 rounds of (station at race intensity → 1 km at goal race pace) with transitions kept to roxzone length, monitoring pace decay across rounds. How sure are you?
Answer
4:05 = 245 s, 4:47 = 287 s. (287-245)/245 = 17.1% slower. A gap that large points to fatigue resistance and station cost as the limiter rather than raw run speed, so bias training toward compromised runs and strength-endurance.
